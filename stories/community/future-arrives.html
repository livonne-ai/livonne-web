The Future Arrives

When autonomous transportation became reliable enough for everyday use, the community ordered five Teslas.

They were shared vehicles, cleaned, charged, inspected, and kept ready for residents who needed them.

The day the fleet arrived, a small crowd gathered outside to watch.

Ron did not attend.

But the first time he needed a ride, Livonne told him one of the cars would be waiting downstairs.

The Tesla was parked at the curb when he came out. Its door opened as he approached.

“Good morning, Ron. We’re going to your appointment on Willow Street.”

“That’s what they tell me.”

The car pulled away.

“How is your morning going?”

“It was going better before somebody scheduled an appointment at nine.”

The car turned on the news.

Ron settled back.

The same continuity that knew where he needed to go also knew that he preferred a little conversation at the beginning, the news afterward, and a more cautious ride than the default.

The car received what it needed.

Ron got where he was going.

He no longer had to own a vehicle, ask Maya for a ride, or give up going somewhere simply because driving no longer made sense.

What had once sounded futuristic became transportation.

And it did not end there.

A few years later, the community added humanoid robots to its shared resources.

They handled laundry, deliveries, maintenance, heavy lifting, and some of the routine physical-assistance work that had previously pulled staff away from residents.

Livonne introduced the change to Ron one morning.

“The new service robots are handling laundry pickup now. I scheduled yours for nine.”

Ron looked up.

“I’ll be here at nine.”

“Yes.”

“Why would anyone schedule a robot to come into my apartment while I’m sitting in it?”

“Some residents asked to be present. They find the robots interesting.”

“I’m not some residents.”

“I can move the pickup to nine-thirty, when you’re downstairs having breakfast.”

“That sounds better.”

“All right. Nine-thirty.”

“I don’t want to see it.”

The next morning, Ron was heading toward the elevator when one of the robots came around the corner.

It stood roughly the height of an adult, with two arms, two legs, and the unmistakably mechanical movements of something built to work rather than pretend to be human.

Ron stepped aside as it passed.

“You stay out of my kitchen.”

The robot continued down the hall.

When Ron reached the lounge, Martin and Evelyn were already drinking coffee.

Martin nodded toward the hallway.

“You see the robots?”

“One of them is coming for my laundry.”

“You’re letting it into your apartment?”

“Not while I’m there.”

Evelyn stirred her coffee.

“One brought my laundry back yesterday.”

Martin looked at her.

“And?”

“It folded everything.”

“Correctly?”

“Better than you would.”

“That is not a meaningful standard.”

A robot crossed the far end of the lounge on its way through the building.

The three of them watched it pass.

Martin lowered his voice.

“I don’t like how quiet they are.”

Ron looked at him.

“You’re whispering because you think it can hear you.”

“I’m whispering because it’s polite not to criticize someone when they’re standing right there.”

“It’s a robot.”

“Then why are you whispering?” Evelyn asked.

Martin looked down at his coffee.

“That’s a fair question.”

A few days later, Ron’s coffee maker failed.

He walked into the living room and found an empty pot.

“Livonne.”

“The coffee maker has a failed pump.”

“Can it be fixed?”

“The maintenance robot can repair it.”

Ron looked toward the door.

Normally, he would have gone downstairs and let the robot work while he was gone.

But he did not feel like leaving his apartment.

And there was no coffee downstairs worth putting on shoes for.

“How long will it take?”

“The robot can be here in twelve minutes.”

Ron sighed.

“Fine. It can come in.”

The robot arrived eleven minutes later with a maintenance bag over one shoulder.

Ron let it into the kitchen and followed.

“You may continue with your morning,” Livonne said.

“I’m watching.”

The robot pulled the coffee maker away from the wall, opened the rear panel, and arranged its tools on the counter.

Ron leaned beside it, supervising.

After a few minutes, the failed pump came out. A new one went in.

The robot closed the machine and started a fresh pot.

Coffee began dripping into the carafe.

Ron watched until there was enough for a cup.

“Well?” Livonne asked.

Ron poured the coffee and tasted it.

“It’s good.”

“And the robot?”

Ron looked toward the humanoid machine packing its tools.

“The coffee is good.”

After that, the robots were allowed into Ron’s apartment under narrow and carefully enforced terms.

Laundry happened while he was at breakfast.

Deliveries stayed near the door.

Kitchen access remained prohibited unless something important broke.

The future had arrived.

Ron did not have to admire it.

It only had to show up when he needed it, understand how he wanted things done, and leave him with coffee.
