Three weeks later, Ron’s chair arrived at the apartment overlooking the pond.

It came through the door sideways, carried by two movers who disagreed about whether it would fit until Ron explained that it had already fit through four other doors and none of those had required a committee.

Once it was in place, he lowered himself into it and looked around.

The room was still mostly boxes. Maya was unpacking the kitchen. Someone was rolling a cart of clothes toward the bedroom.

Then the Livonne Surface came to life.

“Hey, Ron.”

The voice was exactly where he had left it.

“I see you got the chair.”

“Finally. Someone here understands the order of operations.”

“Chair first. Coffee second.”

“That’s right.”

A pause.

“Although I don’t see a coffee maker.”

Ron looked toward the kitchen.

Maya was surrounded by newspaper, dishes, and three boxes that all claimed to contain kitchen supplies.

“We can fix that,” Livonne said. “The west lounge has coffee.”

Ron remembered the lounge from the tour.

“Oh, right. The community center.”

“The west lounge is two minutes away. Fred is there now.”

Maya looked up.

“I can find the coffee maker.”

“You find the kitchen,” Ron said. “I’ll inspect the coffee.”

He reached for his Care Band.

“Do you want directions?” Livonne asked.

“I know where it is.”

“Of course.”

Ron fastened the band around his wrist and headed for the door.

The shared Surface near the elevator recognized him as he approached. It brought up the route to the west lounge, along with a reminder that the college-football barbecue started at five-thirty.

Ron ignored both and pressed the elevator button.

He knew where he was going.

Mostly.

Fred was sitting near the lounge window with two cups already on the table.

“They let you move in?” he asked.

“Apparently the screening process needs work.”

Fred pushed one of the cups toward him.

“I heard you were coming.”

Ron took a sip.

It was strong enough to be useful.

For the next twenty minutes, they resumed the football argument they had started during the tour.

A woman passing their table stopped when she heard Fred defending his team’s offensive line.

“You two talking professional football?” she asked.

“Trying to,” Ron said. “He keeps interfering.”

She laughed.

“I gave up on the pros. College football is better. At least the mistakes are more interesting.”

“That’s a generous way to describe bad football,” Ron said.

She pointed toward the poster on the far wall.

“We’re watching the early game at the barbecue tonight. You should come.”

“I just moved in.”

“Then you don’t have an excuse yet.”

Someone called to her from the hallway.

She gave Ron her name before leaving, but it disappeared almost immediately beneath another complaint from Fred about the upcoming season.

By the time Ron returned to the apartment, Maya had found the coffee maker, the filters, and every coffee cup except the one he actually used.

Livonne greeted him as he walked in.

“How was the coffee?”

“Pretty good. Fred was there.”

“I know.”

“And some woman stopped by. She likes college football.”

“That was Evelyn.”

Ron stopped.

“That’s right.”

“Evelyn makes her name and a few interests visible to other residents. You’ve both allowed Livonne to remember introductions made in shared spaces.”

Ron took off his Care Band and placed it beside the chair.

“That could be useful.”

“She has already RSVP’d for the college-football barbecue tonight.”

Maya emerged from the kitchen holding two mismatched mugs.

“I can stay and have dinner with you.”

Ron looked toward the Surface.

“What time did you say?”

“Five-thirty.”

“And what are they serving?”

“Burgers, potato salad, and grilled vegetables.”

“No meatloaf?”

“Not tonight.”

Ron nodded.

“It’s not the NFL, but it’s better than Thursday meatloaf.”

Maya smiled.

“Sounds like you already have plans.”

“That’s what I’ve been trying to tell you.”

“You have not been trying to tell me that.”

“I’m telling you now.”

She set the mugs on the counter.

“Call me if you need anything.”

“I have Livonne.”

“Call me anyway.”

“I’ll think about it.”

Maya hugged him, collected her purse, and finally left.

At five twenty-five, Ron put his Care Band back on and walked downstairs.

The barbecue had spread from the lounge onto the patio. The game was already playing on the large shared Surface. The score occupied one corner while the community schedule and food service remained available along the side.

When Ron approached, the Surface briefly showed him where Evelyn was sitting and where the open seats were.

Nothing else changed.

No announcement. No spotlight. Just enough help to keep him from entering a crowded room without knowing where to go.

Evelyn saw him first.

“You made it.”

“I was promised football.”

“You were warned it was college football.”

She moved her purse from the chair beside her.

Ron sat down.

A few minutes later, a man across the table objected loudly to a targeting call.

“That’s not targeting,” he said. “That’s called tackling.”

Ron looked at the replay.

“He lowered his helmet.”

“He lowered his shoulder.”

“He doesn’t know where his shoulder is.”

The man stared at Ron for a second.

Then he laughed.

“Martin.”

“Ron.”

By halftime, they had resolved nothing.

They had, however, covered football rules, bad officiating, the decline of defensive play, and whether instant replay had improved anything.

Evelyn mostly watched the game and corrected both of them when they became too confident.

Ron stayed through the fourth quarter.

When he returned to his apartment, the boxes were still there.

But the room no longer felt quite as empty.

The next morning, he woke before the room made sense.

The window was in the wrong place. The hallway stretched in the wrong direction. For a few seconds, he could not remember where the bathroom was.

The Bedside came to life.

“Good morning, Ron. You’re in your new apartment at Livonne Community. The bathroom is through the bedroom, on your left.”

Ron sat up and looked around.

“Right. I knew that.”

“I know.”

A few minutes later, he walked into the living room.

“All right. Where’s the coffee?”

“The coffee is ready.”

Ron stopped.

A fresh pot was waiting in the automated coffee maker.

“Did you make it?”

“This time I did, Ron. The community allows me to connect directly to the coffee maker. So yes. I made your coffee this morning.”

Ron poured himself a cup and took a sip.

“Well. Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

He settled into his chair.

After a moment, Livonne asked, “How was the barbecue?”

“Food was good.”

“And the company?”

“Evelyn knows football. Fred talks too much. Met another fellow named Martin.”

“Martin’s public community profile says he plays chess at the pond on Tuesday afternoons.”

Ron looked up.

“He any good?”

“It doesn’t say.”

“Probably not, then.”

“Would you like me to ask whether he wants a game?”

Ron considered it.

That was the part that used to disappear.

A name heard once. An interest discovered in passing. The vague intention to talk again, buried beneath everything else before it could become a plan.

“Tell him Tuesday,” Ron said. “Two o’clock.”

Livonne sent the invitation.

A moment later, the answer came back.

“Martin says Tuesday at two works for him. He’ll meet you at the pond.”

“Tell him not to expect mercy.”

“I’ll let you handle that part.”

The day before, Martin had been a stranger across a table.

Now someone was expecting Ron on Tuesday.

Livonne had remembered the connection, found the opening, and handled the awkward little piece of coordination that so often kept a promising conversation from becoming anything more.

The rest was up to Ron.
