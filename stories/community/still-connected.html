A few weeks after Ron moved into the community, his Surface lit up while he was finishing breakfast.

“Sarah has a soccer game this afternoon,” Livonne said. “Would you like to wish her luck?”

Ron looked up from his coffee.

“What time?”

“Three-thirty.”

“Is Maya taking her?”

“Yes.”

“Then tell Sarah good luck. And remind her to keep her head up when she kicks.”

“Would you like to record that?”

Ron cleared his throat.

“Hey, Sarah. Good luck today. Keep your head up when you kick, and don’t listen to your mother if she tells you I said that. Love you, buddy.”

Livonne sent the message.

Across town, Sarah received it while she was getting ready for school.

Ron did not see that part.

He did not need to.

The message had reached her without Maya remembering to arrange a call, without Ron trying to work through three different apps, and without anyone turning it into an event.

It was simply something a grandfather should know.

Later that afternoon, Ron was at the pond losing a chess game to Martin.

Martin studied the board.

“You’re in trouble.”

“I’m thinking.”

“You’ve been thinking for five minutes.”

“That’s why you should be worried.”

Ron’s Care Band gave a gentle pulse.

Sarah’s game had started.

He glanced at it, then returned to the board.

Livonne would let him know if there was something worth seeing. He did not need to sit in his apartment refreshing a score or asking Maya for updates.

Martin moved his bishop.

Ron stared at the board.

“That’s an irritating move.”

“It’s a good move.”

“Those aren’t mutually exclusive.”

By the time Ron returned to his apartment, the game was over.

His Surface showed a single new family update.

Sarah’s team had won two to one.

There were five photographs.

Not every picture Maya had taken. Not a hundred nearly identical shots from the sideline. Just the few that carried the afternoon.

Sarah lining up a kick.

Sarah running down the field.

Her team gathered around the coach.

A blurry photograph of everyone celebrating.

And one final picture of Sarah smiling in the back seat on the way home.

A short message appeared beneath them.

Grandpa, we won! I kept my head up.

Ron laughed.

“Send her a picture back.”

“Would you like me to pull a still from the path security camera?”

“Of what?”

“You and Martin playing chess.”

“Do I look like I’m winning?”

“In one of them.”

“Use that one. Tell her I won too.”

“You lost to Martin.”

“She doesn’t need every detail.”

Livonne sent the photograph and message.

The family connection worked in both directions.

Livonne could catch the moments worth sharing—a soccer game, a chess match at the pond, something funny a child or pet did at home—and carry them across the distance while they were still fresh.

The family did not have to stop living long enough to document its life. Livonne simply helped the good moments reach the people who would care about them.

A few days later, Ron’s Surface brought up another message.

“Maya wants to know whether you’re free Thursday evening.”

“What does she need?”

“She would like to try a family game night.”

Ron narrowed his eyes.

“What kind of game?”

“Charades.”

“No.”

“Sarah has already chosen teams.”

“That seems premature.”

“She put you on hers.”

Ron considered that.

“Who did Connor get?”

“His father.”

Ron smiled.

“All right. But tell Sarah I’m not doing anything embarrassing.”

“I’ll pass that along.”

On Thursday evening, Ron carried his coffee into the living room.

At seven, the Surface changed.

The usual calendar and community information moved aside. The screen opened into a tall video window, large enough that Maya’s family room appeared almost full-sized on the other side.

Maya was still trying to get everyone into position.

Connor was arguing about the rules.

Sarah was waving at the screen.

James was asking whether anyone had decided how to keep score.

For the next hour, Ron’s apartment filled with the ordinary noise of his family.

People talked over one another.

Someone disputed a point.

Sarah accused Connor of cheating.

Ron’s team won, although Connor maintained that the scoring system had been compromised from the beginning.

Then came the goodnights.

The family room disappeared, and Ron’s apartment returned.

For a moment, the room was quiet again.

But it was not the same quiet he had known at home.

There, family connection had depended on someone remembering to call, finding a time that worked, and staying on long enough to make the effort feel worthwhile.

Here, Livonne could keep the small threads from disappearing.

A soccer game.

A message before school.

Five photographs after the final whistle.

An invitation that might otherwise have stayed an idea.

A family room opening across the distance for an hour.

Livonne did not replace the visit.

It helped more of the life between visits survive.

Ron finished his coffee and looked at the darkened screen.

“Remind me to ask Sarah about her next game.”

“I will.”

“And don’t tell anyone Martin beat me.”

“Your secret is safe.”

Ron had moved away from the family home.

He had not moved out of the family.
