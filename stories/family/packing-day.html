The house felt empty without Ron in it.

His chair was gone. The space where it had sat looked larger than Maya remembered, as if the room had already begun forgetting how it used to be arranged.

She carried in a box, set it on the floor, and walked toward the Livonne Surface.

As she approached, her caregiver dashboard appeared.

Every protection was green.

A short activity log sat beneath it. One kitchen reminder earlier in the week. Resolved. No escalation needed.

Everything was fine.

“Hey, Maya,” Livonne said. “How’s it going?”

She stopped.

“Oh. Hey. I thought you moved.”

“My primary operations moved with your dad. His assistant, private memory, preferences, and routines are active in his apartment at the Livonne Community.”

“So what are you doing here?”

“Part of my continuity belongs to you. Your preferences. Your permissions. The coordination history you built while helping your father. This Surface also remains functional until you decide what to do with it.”

Maya looked around the room.

“I was thinking I’d sell it. We sold the lift bed. Some of the other equipment is being picked up tomorrow. I don’t need memory care.”

“No,” Livonne said. “But you already use the Surface Mini at home.”

“To help coordinate Dad’s life.”

“Right. It kept appointments, reminders, changes, and follow-up from living entirely in your head.”

“That was helpful.”

“What if I did that for the rest of your family?”

Maya looked back at the Surface.

“What do you mean?”

“Livonne Family.”

The dashboard changed.

Not into a product catalog. Just a simple picture of her household: four people, a shared home, and the places their lives moved through every day.

“I could help coordinate the things your family already manages,” Livonne said. “Schedules, rides, school forms, meals, household routines, and the changes that currently disappear into separate calendars and text messages.”

“So the whole family uses this screen?”

“The Surface becomes the shared household layer. But family life doesn’t happen in front of one screen.”

The picture expanded.

Phones. The car. The smaller Surface already in Maya’s home.

“In a family setting, I travel through personal apps and approved devices. Each person has their own assistant, preferences, and private space. The household shares only what needs to be shared.”

“And Dad’s information?”

“Stays with Dad.”

“You wouldn’t bring his memory into our house.”

“No. The hardware can be reset without transferring his private continuity. His history is his. Yours is yours.”

Maya considered that.

“And we wouldn’t need the same monitoring setup he had.”

“No. Livonne Family operates differently. Shared surfaces can remain quiet until someone approaches. Personal context stays with the person. Household protections still work locally, but the family does not become a care subject.”

That distinction mattered.

Not her father’s system installed in another house.

The same foundation, doing a different job.

Maya reached behind the Surface.

“Okay,” she said. “Let’s take you home.”

A few days later, James and Connor carried the larger Surface through Maya’s front door.

“Careful,” she said.

“We’ve got it,” James answered.

“You said that before you hit the doorframe.”

“That was controlled contact.”

Connor was walking backward with the lower end.

“Can we finish this before we review the incident?”

Sarah watched from the stairs.

“Why is Grandpa’s big Livonne here?”

“We’re trying something,” Maya said. “Help us plug it in, and she’ll explain.”

They mounted the larger Surface upstairs, where everyone passed between the bedrooms, kitchen, and front door. The Surface Mini moved downstairs to the family room.

The kids already knew the Mini. It had been part of Maya’s life while she coordinated Ron’s care. The larger Surface was familiar too, although seeing it in their own hallway made it feel different.

Maya turned it on.

“Hey, Maya.”

Livonne paused.

“New house.”

Connor stepped closer.

“How can you tell?”

“Different rooms. Different network. Considerably more traffic.”

“That’s him,” Sarah said.

“Her,” Connor corrected.

“It’s a computer.”

“You can each decide how I sound and appear when I’m helping you,” Livonne said.

Connor’s expression changed immediately.

“Can mine be an orc king?”

“Almost certainly.”

“A level-seven magi orc king?”

“I don’t know what makes an orc king level seven, but we can work through it.”

Sarah shook her head.

“I’m not talking to an orc.”

“You don’t have to. Your assistant can look and sound different.”

Maya folded her arms.

“And mine?”

“You can have the house elf you always wanted.”

James looked at her.

“You wanted a house elf?”

“I wanted someone else to notice when we were out of laundry detergent.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is emotionally the same thing.”

James turned toward the Surface.

“Can mine just be normal?”

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

Maya gathered everyone closer.

“Here’s the deal. Livonne Family gives us shared help for things that affect the household. Calendars, rides, plans, reminders, all of that. But each of us also gets a personal space.”

Sarah looked at the Surface.

“So Mom doesn’t see everything?”

“No,” Livonne said. “Shared information is shared. Personal information stays personal unless you choose to share it or it crosses a safety rule the family has agreed to.”

“No secret transcripts?”

“No secret transcripts.”

Sarah looked at Maya.

“That’s one of my rules too,” Maya said.

James nodded toward the Surface.

“How long are we trying this?”

“A month,” Maya said.

Connor shrugged.

“Sure.”

Sarah nodded.

“As long as his orc stays upstairs.”

“My orc goes where I go.”

“That is not helping your case.”

They started with names, voices, and what each person wanted shared with the household.

Connor began designing something with horns.

Sarah rejected six voices before choosing one.

James selected the default.

Maya connected the family calendar.

Within a few minutes, the Surface stopped looking like equipment from Ron’s house.

Downstairs, the kids were still arguing about whether an orc king could also be a magician. James was looking for the phone charger someone had moved. Tomorrow’s schedule was beginning to assemble quietly on the shared screen.

Ron’s Livonne had gone with him.

This one belonged to them.
