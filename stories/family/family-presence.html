On the morning of Sarah’s soccer game, her phone buzzed while she was packing her bag.

“Your grandpa sent you a message.”

She opened it.

Ron appeared on the screen.

“Hey, Sarah. Good luck today. Keep your head up when you kick, and don’t listen to your mother if she tells you I said that. Love you, buddy.”

Sarah laughed.

Maya looked over from the doorway.

“What?”

“Grandpa says not to listen to you.”

“That could apply to almost anything.”

“He says to keep my head up when I kick.”

“That part you can listen to.”

Sarah slipped the phone into her bag.

“Did you remind him about the game?”

“No.”

That was the part Maya was still getting used to.

For years, she had been the connection between Ron and everyone else. If he needed to know about a game, an appointment, or something happening with the kids, Maya had to remember to tell him.

Now Ron could remain part of the family’s life without Maya arranging every interaction herself.

At the game, Sarah’s team won two to one.

Maya took pictures from the sideline.

Sarah lining up a kick.

The team laughing on the bench.

A blurry shot of everyone celebrating after the final whistle.

As they walked back toward the car, Sarah’s Livonne app opened.

“You got some good pictures today. Would you like to send a few to your grandpa?”

Sarah looked through them.

“Yeah.”

She removed one photograph of herself halfway through a sneeze.

“Not that one.”

Livonne kept four others.

“Would you like to add a message?”

Sarah held up the phone.

“Grandpa, we won two to one. And I kept my head up.”

The photos and message went to Ron.

Easy as that.

No family group chat. No sorting through every picture later. No expectation that Maya would remember to send an update after loading the chairs, the bags, and the children back into the car.

The moment simply reached him while it was still fresh.

That evening, Maya approached the Surface.

“Anything from Dad?”

“Ron shared a family update.”

A photograph appeared.

Ron was sitting beside the pond with another resident. A chessboard rested between them.

He was leaning back in his chair wearing the expression he used whenever he believed he had already won.

The message beneath it read:

Found myself a chess partner. Tell Sarah I won too.

Maya smiled.

“Did he actually win?”

“No.”

“I didn’t think so.”

Sarah came down the stairs.

“Is that Grandpa?”

Maya showed her the picture.

“He says he won.”

Sarah studied the board.

“He’s about to lose his queen.”

“Apparently Martin neglected to mention that.”

Sarah sent a reply.

Nice try, Grandpa.

Over the following weeks, more moments moved between the two homes.

Connor sent Ron a picture of the first replacement part he sold through the Livonne Network.

Ron responded with a video message.

“Twenty dollars is too cheap.”

Connor watched as Ron continued.

“And don’t worry about the first two that broke. The one that fits is the one you made.”

Connor smiled.

“Livonne, change the price on the next one to thirty dollars.”

“Updated.”

Connor looked back at Ron’s image.

“Great advice, Grandpa.”

Another morning, Maya approached the Surface with her coffee.

Family Presence had three new moments from Ron.

A picture of him walking through the Community garden.

A short clip of Martin accusing him of moving a chess piece while nobody was looking.

And a photograph of Ron eating lunch with four other residents, all of them laughing at something beyond the edge of the frame.

Maya stopped at that one.

She had worried that Ron would move into the Community and spend his days waiting for the family to visit.

Instead, she could see pieces of a life continuing without her.

Friends.

Arguments.

Bad chess.

Ordinary afternoons.

The family did not need a constant window into Ron’s apartment. They only needed enough to know that he was still out there living.

And Ron received the same from them.

A soccer game.

A finished project.

A joke Connor would have told him if they still lived nearby.

The moments were small.

That was why they mattered.
