A week before summer break, Maya approached the Surface.

“Hey, Livonne. Remember the needs network Dad used?”

“The Livonne Network.”

“Right. Are there any needs around the neighborhood that Sarah and Connor could help with this summer? Something that could be a first job?”

“I found two possibilities nearby. A family is looking for an occasional babysitter. Another household needs someone to mow the lawn once a week.”

Maya called the kids over.

Sarah looked at the opportunities on the screen.

“You volunteered us?”

“I found options.”

“That sounds like volunteering with extra steps.”

“Both jobs pay,” Livonne said.

Sarah stepped closer.

“How much?”

The babysitting job paid by the hour. The lawn paid a fixed amount each week.

Connor studied the lawn.

“How big is the yard?”

“About a quarter acre. The mower is provided.”

“Front and back?”

“Yes.”

He thought about it.

“I’ll try it.”

Sarah opened the babysitting request.

Two children. Friday evening. The family lived a few blocks away and already used Livonne Family.

“I’ll meet them,” she said.

Livonne arranged the introductions.

The following Friday, Sarah arrived for her first babysitting night.

The children’s parents met her at the door.

“Dinner’s in the refrigerator,” their mother said. “Bedtime is eight-thirty. Ellie will try to negotiate that.”

A little girl looked up from the floor.

“No, I won’t.”

Her mother continued.

“Ben likes a story before bed. No peanuts anywhere near him. Livonne has the rest of the notes and our contact information.”

The Surface lit as Sarah approached.

“Hey, Sarah. I’ve got everything they shared for tonight.”

The parents said goodbye and left.

Dinner went smoothly.

Ben ate most of his food. Ellie ate enough to claim that she had.

While they built a tower on the family-room floor, Livonne captured a picture of all three of them laughing as it collapsed. The parents received it while they were waiting for dinner.

A little later, another photo arrived.

Both children at the table. Dinner underway. Everyone fine.

At eight-thirty, Ellie began negotiations.

“Our normal bedtime is nine.”

“The notes say eight-thirty.”

“That’s an old note.”

“It was updated tonight.”

Ellie considered this.

“My mom worries too much.”

“She does seem thorough.”

Sarah got them upstairs.

Ben refused to get into bed.

Livonne gave her a quiet prompt.

Story first.

Sarah picked up the book beside him.

“Okay. One story.”

“Two.”

“One long one.”

Ben accepted the deal.

By the time she finished, he was under the blankets. Ellie had stopped arguing and was listening from the hallway.

When the parents returned, the house was quiet.

“How did it go?” their mother asked.

“Good,” Sarah said.

And it actually had.

Connor’s first lawn job was less complicated.

Livonne gave him the address and the scheduled time. The homeowner showed him where the mower was, pointed out the parts of the yard he wanted done, and left Connor to it.

The work was hot, repetitive, and paid considerably better than taking out the trash.

The next week, Connor went back.

His second lawn job turned into something else.

He was packing up when he noticed the homeowner crouched near the garage, holding a broken plastic bracket.

“I’m never going to find another one of these,” the man said.

Connor looked at it.

“I could probably print you one.”

The man glanced up.

“You can?”

“Probably.”

“I’ll give you twenty dollars if you can figure it out.”

Connor took the bracket home.

He measured it in the garage, built a model on his computer, and sent it to the printer.

The first version was too tight.

The second snapped when he tried to install it.

The third fit.

The next afternoon, Connor handed it to the homeowner.

The man pressed it into place and gave it a pull.

“That’s pretty slick.”

Connor smiled.

“Just like Grandpa.”

The homeowner added the repair to his Livonne Network review.

After that, more requests began finding Connor.

A neighbor needed a replacement clip.

Someone else needed a wall mount.

Another person had a plastic part nobody could identify and no store carried.

Connor took the jobs one at a time.

Before long, his lawn-mowing profile had become something else: a small neighborhood service for replacement parts and simple fabrication.

One evening, James stepped into the garage.

Connor was standing over the printer while another finished part sat on the workbench.

“Dinner’s getting cold,” James said.

“I’ll be right in. I had to restart this one.”

James looked around.

Several orders were waiting beside the computer. Names, measurements, and pickup times were written on scraps of paper.

“You’ve got a lot going on.”

Connor looked at the printer.

“Yeah. It might actually be something.”

“I think it is.”

James picked up one of the notes.

“You want some help turning this into a real business?”

Connor looked over.

“What do you mean?”

“Track the orders. Materials. Payments. Deadlines. Keep all of this from turning into a pile of paper in the garage.”

Connor looked at the workbench.

“That already happened.”

“Then we’re just in time.”

After dinner, they sat down together.

Livonne helped them build a simple workspace.

Orders.

Materials.

Payments.

Pickup times.

Nothing Connor did not need.

Then James leaned back.

“One dad suggestion.”

Connor waited.

“Half of what you make goes into savings for the school year.”

Connor thought for a moment.

“That’s probably smart.”

James smiled.

“I was expecting more resistance.”

“I need more filament. I can’t spend everything.”

They set up the savings rule.

A few minutes later, Sarah’s payment from babysitting arrived.

Connor looked at the notification.

“How much did you make?”

“None of your business.”

“I have an actual business.”

“I kept two actual children alive.”

James stood up from the table.

“She wins this round.”

Sarah smiled.
